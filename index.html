Introduction · The world's mammals at a glance

Over six thousand mammals. Every shape of life.

Around 6,400 described mammal species roam our planet, split across 27 orders and every biome — from the frozen Arctic to the red heart of the Simpson Desert. They range in size from a 30-millimetre bat to a 30-metre whale, and live between two years and two centuries. Mammals are the group we belong to — but the mammals we know best in Australia are strange even by mammal standards.

The three species-rich orders we see globally — rodents, bats and eulipotyphlans (shrew-relatives) like shrews — account for over 70% of all mammal species. Yet Australia has evolved an entirely different cast of characters: egg-laying monotremes, pouch-rearing marsupials, and a small set of placental species that arrived on their own terms.

Before we dive into what makes Australia's mammals special, let's put the world's mammals in perspective.

World · Choropleth Map

An island apart

A world choropleth of mammal endemism by country. Australia, Madagascar and the Philippines sit at the top of the leaderboard.

Why do islands lead? Species that cannot cross an ocean cannot spread, and little can easily reach them. Over millions of years island populations diverge from their relatives elsewhere and become unique. That is why Australia, Madagascar and the Philippines top the map, while countries on large connected landmasses score lower.

02

Eggs and pouches

Two of the three great branches of mammal life — egg-laying monotremes and pouch-rearing marsupials — are overwhelmingly concentrated in the Australian region.

The strangest mammals on Earth

All five living monotreme species in the world (the platypus and four species of echidna) are found only in Australia and New Guinea. Monotremes and marsupials represent two of the three great mammal lineages; about two-thirds of marsupial species, and all monotremes, live in Australasia.

Where most of the world's mammals gave up the pouch and the egg, Australian mammals turned both into evolutionary adaptations. The platypus, echidna, kangaroo, wombat and koala evolved in isolation over tens of millions of years after Australia separated from Antarctica (about 35-45 million years ago).

Australia's mammals didn't merely survive isolation — they built a different, stranger biology within it.

Body mass by higher group

Violin plot of log(body mass) for Australian monotremes, marsupials and placentals. Marsupial body masses spread wider than monotremes but stop short of the placental giants.

03

How they feed, move & sleep

Australia's mammals fill ecological roles seen nowhere else: tree-dwelling leaf-eaters, flying nectar specialists, and nocturnal burrowers.

Chart idiom · Heatmap

What they eat and how they get around

Heatmap of diet category vs. movement mode for Australian mammal species. Each square counts species sharing that combination.

Key ecological patterns: The aerial guild consists mainly of insectivorous bats and frugivorous flying-foxes. Ground-dwelling species are dominated by insect-eaters (dunnarts, bandicoots, echidnas) and plant-eaters (kangaroos, wallabies). Tree-dwelling herbivores like the koala adapt to low-nutrient eucalyptus foliage, a dietary niche few other mammals can exploit.

Chart idiom · Dumbbell Chart

Day vs. night activity

Proportion of diurnal (active during day) vs. nocturnal (active during night) species across Australian families.

Why nocturnal dominance? Much of Australia is arid and hot. Sheltering in burrows or tree hollows by day and foraging at night conserves vital water. Daytime specialists such as the numbat (which feeds on daytime-active termites) and the musky rat-kangaroo represent rare evolutionary exceptions.

Threat category definitions: Categories progress by severity — Conservation Dependent (survives only with active management), Vulnerable (high risk of endangerment), Endangered (very high risk of extinction in the wild), and Critically Endangered (extremely high risk of extinction).

05

Under pressure

Endemism cuts both ways. Australia's mammals are unique — and uniquely vulnerable. Around 9% of native species (35 species) have gone extinct since European settlement, and 63 are currently listed as threatened.

Chart idiom · Normalised Bar chart

Which mammal groups have lost the most species?

Share of each major Australian mammal order classified as living vs. extinct since European settlement.

Vulnerability factors: European settlement introduced invasive predators (feral cats and red foxes) and altered fire regimes. Extinctions concentrated among ground-dwelling mammals within the 35 g to 5.5 kg 'critical weight range' (such as bandicoots and native rodents). Flying mammals (bats) suffered almost no extinctions.

The cost of being different

Australia has experienced the highest modern mammal extinction rate of any continent. 35 species (~9% of native mammal fauna) have disappeared in the last 500 years, and a further 63 are currently listed as threatened (Woinarski et al., 2015).

Primary drivers include introduced predators, land clearing, and altered fire regimes. Endemism amplifies these risks — a species restricted to a single geographic area can be devastated by localized habitat loss or predator expansion.

Preserving Australia's mammals means preserving the very strangeness that makes them worth studying.
